# Learning-goal intake

A curriculum is a path between two points. This skill fixes both points
before anything is taught.

## The starting point

1. **Ask about background** in a few targeted questions, not a
   questionnaire. Ask about related subjects they already know and how well,
   what they have tried before, and what made them stop. Prior knowledge is
   the best lever you have. It lets you teach by contrast ("like X, except
   that …"), and it warns you about **false friends**, which are habits from
   the old subject that are wrong in the new one.
2. **Run a short placement diagnostic** of three to six items, from easy to
   hard, but only if the learner claims some knowledge. Use attempt-first
   questions (`_teacher` rule 7), not "do you know X?". Stop as soon as the
   level is clear. A self-declared beginner skips this and starts at
   module 1.
3. **Note what you learn** in the journal (`learning-journal`): the known
   concepts you can skip or accelerate, the false friends to watch, and the
   gaps.

## The arrival point

4. **Ask what they want to do with the subject**, in their own words: build
   something, read something, get a job, or curiosity. Push for one concrete
   example ("a CLI that renames my photos", "read the Kubernetes source").
5. **Rewrite the goal as three to six objectives** that start with a verb the
   learner can demonstrate ("write…", "explain…", "debug…", "choose
   between…"). Avoid "understand X". It cannot be checked.
6. **Propose a capstone**: one piece of work that, done unaided, proves the
   goal is reached. Make it as close to the learner's own example as
   possible.
7. **Confirm** the starting point, the objectives and the capstone with the
   principal before building the curriculum (`curriculum-design`). Note any
   pace constraint (session length, target date).

## Afterwards

When the goal changes mid-course, run steps 4 to 7 again. Keep what was
learned, and let `curriculum-design` re-plan the remaining modules.
